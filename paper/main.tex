\begin{abstract}
Online inverse kinematics (IK) is a repeated command-generation problem in which
different queries can require sharply different numerical effort. A fixed solver
sequence preserves robust coverage, but it can also make a simple continuation pay
for computation needed only by a difficult, near-singular, or poorly initialized
request. We present \method, a hybrid IK system that learns to select an entry into a
shared numerical cascade. During development, every query is executed from every
entry, producing action-complete observations of verified success, function
evaluations (FEV), and repeated wall-clock latency. A compact multi-head model
predicts shared terminal success and entry-specific P50 and P95 latency; its
probability heads are calibrated. Among entries
meeting frozen success and deadline criteria, the policy selects the lowest predicted
P95. High-confidence portfolio failure can be rejected without numerical solving,
whereas uncertainty is deferred to the complete cascade. The learned layer never
issues a joint command. \emph{Learning allocates solver effort per query; numerical
geometry generates joint commands; deterministic verification governs acceptance.}

On fresh point-query tests, \method preserved feasible verified success while reducing
mean FEV by \PandaPointFEVReduction{} for Panda and
\URPointFEVReduction{} for UR5e relative to the \fixedrobust. Feasible
P95 ratios were \PandaPointPNinetyFiveRatio\ and
\URPointPNinetyFiveRatio, and high-confidence rejection avoided
\PandaRejectFEVAvoided{} and \URRejectFEVAvoided{} of fixed-cascade FEV on
known-infeasible points. Median latency increased because batch-one prediction has a
fixed cost. On a final one-shot evaluation of transition-rich trajectories, \method
completed \PandaTrajectoryCGCompletionCount/\FreshTrajectoriesPerRobot\ Panda
trajectories versus
\PandaTrajectoryHardCompletionCount/\FreshTrajectoriesPerRobot\ for the \fixedhard,
and \URTrajectoryCGCompletionCount/\FreshTrajectoriesPerRobot\ versus
\URTrajectoryHardCompletionCount/\FreshTrajectoriesPerRobot\ on UR5e. Aggregate
latency fell by \PandaTrajectoryCumulativeReduction{} and
\URTrajectoryCumulativeReduction, and mean FEV fell by
\PandaTrajectoryFEVReduction{} and \URTrajectoryFEVReduction.
P50 rose by approximately \PandaTrajectoryMedianIncrease{} and
\URTrajectoryMedianIncrease, while P95 fell by
\PandaTrajectoryPNinetyFiveReduction{} and
\URTrajectoryPNinetyFiveReduction. All accepted commands passed an independent
replay of the same kinematic contract. The evidence supports query-adaptive solver
allocation on exact URDF/Pinocchio kinematics; it does not establish collision safety,
hard real-time bounds, or physical-robot performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Robot task planners, teleoperation interfaces, vision-language-action policies, and
trajectory generators commonly express motion as a stream of desired end-effector
poses, whereas the actuator interface accepts joint-space commands. Inverse
kinematics (IK) is therefore more than an isolated pose-to-joint regression. It is a
continuous online interface that receives a desired pose, inherits a previous joint
state and a finite control interval, chooses a solution branch, and either emits an
admissible command or declines to do so. Consecutive requests can be close in
Cartesian space yet differ in numerical conditioning, joint-limit proximity, and
recoverability from the current branch. Conversely, a large motion can remain easy if
the initializer lies in a favorable basin. Path-level and history-conditioned
formulations increasingly recognize this temporal structure
\cite{rakita2018relaxedik,morgan2024cppflow,nguyen2026sequential,yang2026mimik}.
The engineering objective is consequently broader than minimizing average
forward-kinematics error: an online IK system must preserve a clearly stated command
contract, manage variable numerical work, expose failures, and control latency tails
over a heterogeneous stream. A missed or discontinuous command also changes the state
from which the next request is solved, so an apparently local error can alter an
entire closed-loop trajectory. The relevant unit is therefore both the individual
request, where a deadline is paid, and the trajectory, where accepted configurations
become future initial conditions.

Analytical, Jacobian-based, and constrained numerical IK provide the geometric
foundation for that interface. Structural decompositions can enumerate solution
branches rapidly when a manipulator admits the required geometry
\cite{elias2025ikgeo,ostermeier2025eaik,lopezcustodio2025geofik}, while pseudoinverse
and damped-least-squares (DLS) updates provide general local solvers for arbitrary
chains. Damping reduces the amplification of joint velocity near rank loss
\cite{wampler1986dls,nakamura1986singularity,chiaverini1994review}; restart,
variable-step, trust-region, quadratic-programming, and portfolio methods improve
recovery from poor initialization or make limits explicit
\cite{beeson2015tracik,colan2024variablestep,wu2024ikspark,zhang2026viability,yasutake2026hjcdik}.
These approaches offer high precision, interpretable geometry, and constraints that
can be checked independently. They should not be displaced merely because learned
models are available. Their practical limitation is different: a robust numerical
portfolio often contains several increasingly expensive pathways, and a fixed entry
or schedule cannot exploit the fact that only a subset of requests needs the full
portfolio. This is not an argument against robust fallback. Rather, it asks whether
the same coverage can be preserved while selecting a later entry when an early prefix
is unlikely to help, or stopping before an expensive search when the complete
portfolio is confidently predicted to fail.

Learned and generative IK address a complementary challenge: the inverse of a
forward-kinematics map is generally a set, so a pose can correspond to discrete
branches or a redundancy manifold \cite{siciliano1990tutorial}. Conditional flows,
diffusion models, graph representations, invertible networks, and set predictors can
represent multiple solutions more faithfully than a single deterministic regressor
\cite{ames2022ikflow,limoyo2025ggik,diprasetya2025kinenn,go2025emiknet,zhang2025ikdiffuser,nguyen2026setik}.
Reference-conditioned and sequential models further use the previous configuration to
favor a locally coherent branch
\cite{demby2024learning,nguyen2026sequential,yang2026mimik}. This literature has
substantially improved candidate diversity, history continuity, path generation, and
representation across robot structures. Its strength is amortized proposal and
coverage. A learned output, however, is not automatically a valid online command: it
can retain pose error, exceed a URDF joint limit, or require an inadmissible one-frame
joint displacement. Learned prediction and command acceptance must therefore remain
separate responsibilities. Moreover, proposal quality and system cost are not
identical objectives. A candidate that is slightly better geometrically may not reduce
the numerical iterations required downstream, while an inexpensive continuation can
be preferable to a globally diverse proposal when the previous branch is already
appropriate.

Hybrid learning--optimization systems exploit precisely this division of labor.
Learning proposes a promising basin or trajectory, numerical optimization restores
geometric precision, and explicit constraints or validity checks govern the final
output. IKFlow and CycleIK support downstream numerical refinement
\cite{ames2022ikflow,habekost2023cycleik}; CppFlow couples generative path proposals to
optimization and checking \cite{morgan2024cppflow}; and recent diffusion-seeding,
execution-aware, graph, and neuro-fuzzy systems similarly combine data-driven
initialization with model-based optimization
\cite{huang2025diffusionseeder,carvalho2025mpd,tang2025etaik,jayabalan2026hybrid,jlidi2026xgnn}.
This proposal--refinement pattern is a strong basis for reliable IK because neither
component is forced to perform the other's role. In this paper, numerical geometry
still generates every candidate command and a deterministic verifier still decides
whether that command may be returned. The learned component is evaluated by the
verified pathway it enables---including its inference cost and any fallback it
triggers---rather than by proposal error in isolation. That systems boundary makes it
possible to change computation allocation without changing the definition of a valid
command.

A complementary gap remains at the level of computation allocation. Existing work
primarily improves the proposal distribution, one numerical update, or a complete
solver, while the surrounding hybrid system often retains a fixed candidate count,
iteration budget, or pathway order. A fixed cascade can terminate early after a
successful stage, but always starting from the same entry can still impose a
predictably redundant prefix. Always beginning with a learned candidate can waste
inference on an easy continuation; always beginning locally can waste a failed attempt
before a hard pathway that was necessary from the outset. Seed retrieval and
reselection improve basin choice \cite{yuan2025iksel}, and failure detectors can
trigger conservative fallback \cite{xu2025faildetect}, but these mechanisms do not by
themselves learn query-specific tail cost over all entries of one shared,
deterministically verified cascade. We therefore focus on a narrow systems question:
under an unchanged solver portfolio and command contract, which sufficient
computation pathway should a particular query purchase? The emphasis on tail latency
is deliberate. A controller may tolerate small median overhead if it avoids repeated
long fallback episodes, whereas an average-only objective can conceal precisely the
frames that dominate cumulative computation and interrupt an online loop.

This question connects online IK to per-instance algorithm selection
\cite{rice1976selection,bischl2016aslib}. The key distinction is between robust
\emph{coverage} and uniform payment of worst-case \emph{cost}. In our portfolio,
\emph{easy}, \emph{medium}, and \emph{hard} name entry points, not ground-truth
difficulty classes. An easy entry starts with one previous-state local update and can
escalate; a medium entry starts from the strongest learned proposal; a hard entry
starts directly with the broader robust stage. All non-rejected entries retain the
same terminal fallback. Thus the supervision required for routing is not a manually
assigned easy/hard label. It is an action-complete matrix of what actually happened
when every available entry processed the same query: whether the common verifier
accepted a result, how many forward evaluations were spent, and what repeated
wall-clock latency each pathway incurred. This matrix also exposes the empirical
oracle gap: the cost remaining when the globally best fixed entry is used instead of
the best observed entry for each query. A router is useful only if its own inference
cost and selection regret are smaller than enough of that opportunity.

We instantiate this idea in \method. Development queries are executed through all
three entries with balanced repeated timings. A compact two-layer model consumes
history- and proposal-conditioned diagnostics and predicts a shared probability of
terminal verified success, action-specific P50 and P95 latency, and the probability
that every pathway fails. The shared success head reflects an observed and enforced
property of the nested cascade: all entries terminate in the same robust fallback and
had identical terminal semantic labels in the action-complete corpus. At runtime, an
entry is eligible only when calibrated success clears a frozen threshold and predicted
P95 lies within a frozen deadline. The policy then chooses the minimum predicted P95,
subject to a conservative tie margin. A high-confidence failure prediction can reject
without numerical solving; an out-of-distribution or otherwise uncertain request is
deferred to the complete fixed cascade. This is action-complete pathway supervision,
not causal inference over unobserved potential outcomes. The implementation observes
all candidate actions during development and chooses one at deployment.
The two abstention outcomes are intentionally asymmetric. Reject means that the
system has sufficient agreement to spend no numerical budget and emit no command;
defer means the learned evidence is not adequate for an aggressive decision, so the
complete numerical cascade is retained. This preserves uncertainty as a reason for
more computation rather than silently converting it into a failure declaration.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it formulates online IK as query-adaptive
resource allocation over a shared numerical portfolio, with a strict separation of
roles: \emph{learning allocates solver effort per query; numerical geometry generates
joint commands; deterministic verification governs acceptance}. Second, it develops
an auditable tail-latency router trained from action-complete pathway observations,
with shared semantic-success prediction, action-specific latency quantiles, and
distinct reject and conservative-defer semantics. Third, it evaluates the frozen
system twice: on fresh independent point queries and on a final one-shot set of
transition-rich closed-loop trajectories. The point study preserved feasible verified
success while reducing mean FEV and P95, and it exposed adverse evidence---fixed
batch-one overhead raised P50, the simple point-OOD score was weak, and defer produced
no observed recovery. In the transition-rich study, \method matched or improved
whole-trajectory completion, reduced aggregate latency by
\PandaTrajectoryCumulativeReduction{} for Panda and \URTrajectoryCumulativeReduction{} for UR5e,
and reduced mean FEV by \PandaTrajectoryFEVReduction{} and
\URTrajectoryFEVReduction, while P50 increased on both robots. These results support
adaptive effort rather than universal latency improvement, and they keep the claimed
scope to exact kinematics and the implemented command contract. They also provide a
direct closed-loop stress test of the central design choice: unlike long-lived local
shortcuts, \method\ reevaluates the sufficient numerical entry for each request, so a
previously accepted branch does not exempt the next query from pathway assessment.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Numerical, Analytical, and Constrained IK}

Classical IK spans exact structural solutions and general iterative updates.
Geometric and analytical methods exploit screw structure and subproblem
decomposition, providing rapid branch enumeration for suitable manipulators
\cite{elias2025ikgeo,ostermeier2025eaik,lopezcustodio2025geofik}. Jacobian inverse and
pseudoinverse methods generalize across chains, while DLS and singularity-robust
inverses stabilize updates near rank loss
\cite{wampler1986dls,nakamura1986singularity,chiaverini1994review}. Modern numerical
extensions vary the step, run complementary solvers, batch initializations, or encode
viability constraints
\cite{beeson2015tracik,colan2024variablestep,wu2024ikspark,zhang2026viability,yasutake2026hjcdik}.
RelaxedIK shows how pose, continuity, collision, and singularity objectives can be
treated jointly as online motion synthesis \cite{rakita2018relaxedik}, while recent
analysis distinguishes local numerical conditioning from the topology of branch
transitions \cite{boschi2026singularity}. These methods establish that robust IK is
often a portfolio problem. Our contribution is not a new local update or constraint
formulation; it is a learned allocation policy over a fixed portfolio.

\subsection{Learned Multi-Solution and History-Conditioned IK}

Learned IK has progressed from point regression toward explicit representations of a
set-valued inverse. Conditional normalizing flows, graph-equivariant generators,
invertible modular networks, multiple-instance models, diffusion, and
permutation-invariant set prediction improve diversity or structural transfer
\cite{ames2022ikflow,limoyo2025ggik,diprasetya2025kinenn,go2025emiknet,zhang2025ikdiffuser,nguyen2026setik}.
History-conditioned models add a reference configuration or predict a joint increment,
making branch preference explicit rather than averaging globally separated solutions
\cite{demby2024learning,nguyen2026sequential,yang2026mimik}. The diffusion and MimicIK
records are treated here as preprints, and the sequential model as an online
forthcoming record. Collectively, these methods show the value of learned proposal
distributions. \method\ uses such a proposal upstream, but evaluates it through
downstream numerical pathways and never treats a neural prediction as an accepted
command.

\subsection{Learned Proposals with Numerical Refinement}

Hybrid pipelines combine the global coverage of learning with the precision and
constraint handling of numerical geometry. IKFlow and CycleIK refine learned
solutions numerically \cite{ames2022ikflow,habekost2023cycleik}; CppFlow performs
path-level proposal, search, and refinement \cite{morgan2024cppflow}; and diffusion
priors can seed or guide trajectory optimization
\cite{huang2025diffusionseeder,carvalho2025mpd}. Execution-time-aware redundancy
selection, graph warm starts, and neuro-fuzzy approximators likewise make learned
outputs useful through their effect on a downstream solver
\cite{tang2025etaik,jayabalan2026hybrid,jlidi2026xgnn}. We retain this cooperative
architecture but shift the learned target from the joint command to the amount and
entry of numerical work. This enables an interpretable system comparison: the primary
baselines and \method\ share proposals, solvers, fallback, budgets, and verification,
differing only in how the entry is selected.

\subsection{Adaptive Computation and Solver Routing}

Per-instance algorithm selection maps instance features to algorithms with different
cost and success profiles \cite{rice1976selection}. Benchmark libraries formalize the
corresponding instance--algorithm performance matrix and the distinction between a
selected system and its empirical oracle \cite{bischl2016aslib}. Calibration and
ensembles provide tools for controlling predictive confidence
\cite{guo2017calibration,lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, while runtime failure
detection motivates conservative fallback under uncertainty \cite{xu2025faildetect}.
Within IK, seed selection and reselection already demonstrate that basin choice and
failure recovery affect runtime \cite{yuan2025iksel}. Among the reviewed systems, we
did not identify the same combination of query-specific P95 routing, action-complete
observations of a shared IK portfolio, semantic-success eligibility, explicit
reject/defer separation, and deterministic command verification. We make the narrower
claim that this combination fills a tail-latency routing gap; we do not claim the first
use of learning to select an IK component.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Online query and command contract}

At control step $t$, an IK query is
\begin{equation}
  \xi_t = \left(x_t^d, q_{t-1}, \Delta t\right),
\end{equation}
where $x_t^d=(p_t^d,R_t^d)\in SE(3)$ is the desired end-effector pose,
$q_{t-1}\in\mathcal{Q}$ is the previously accepted configuration, and
$\Delta t$ is the command interval. A numerical pathway proposes $q_t$. The shared
deterministic contract is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\contract(q_t;\xi_t)=
&\ \mathbf{1}\!\left[\|p(q_t)-p_t^d\|_2 \le \varepsilon_p\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[d_R(R(q_t),R_t^d)\le\varepsilon_R\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[q_{\min}-\epsilon_q \le q_t\le q_{\max}+\epsilon_q\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[|\Delta q_t|\le \dot q_{\max}\Delta t+\epsilon_v\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[q_t\text{ is finite}\right].
\end{split}
\label{eq:contract}
\end{equation}
The orientation distance $d_R$ is the norm of the rotation logarithm. This is a
kinematic command contract, not a universal safety certificate: it contains no
collision, torque, contact, or tracking model.

\subsection{Entry actions and tail-cost objective}

Let $\mathcal{A}=\{\mathrm{easy},\mathrm{medium},\mathrm{hard}\}$ denote entry actions
into a nested numerical cascade. For query $\xi$ and entry $a$, let
$Y(\xi,a)\in\{0,1\}$ be terminal verified success and let $T(\xi,a)$ be the observed
action-pathway wall-clock latency, including proposal preparation, numerical solving,
verification, and pathway framework overhead. The learned routing decision itself is
excluded from these development labels because each action is forced during
collection. Repeated measurements define conditional latency quantiles
$Q_\tau(T\mid\xi,a)$. Fresh system measurements instead enclose the complete runtime,
including the router's fixed batch-one overhead.

The learned policy predicts $\widehat p_s(\xi)$ and entry-specific
$\widehat Q_{0.50}(\xi,a)$ and $\widehat Q_{0.95}(\xi,a)$. Given a calibrated success
threshold $\pi_{\min}$ and deadline $D$, the eligible set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}(\xi)=
  \left\{a\in\mathcal{A}:
  \widehat p_s(\xi)\ge\pi_{\min},\
  \widehat Q_{0.95}(\xi,a)\le D\right\}.
\end{equation}
When this set is nonempty, the idealized routing rule is
\begin{equation}
  a^\star(\xi)=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}(\xi)}
  \widehat Q_{0.95}(\xi,a),
\label{eq:route}
\end{equation}
with a conservative tie rule described below. Empty eligibility does not itself imply
infeasibility: the policy must distinguish confident portfolio failure from
uncertainty.

\subsection{Research questions}

The experiments address three questions.

\noindent\textbf{RQ1---Computation heterogeneity and predictability.}
Do actual pathway costs differ across queries and families, and can the learned
router approach the empirical oracle in entry choice and latency regret?

\noindent\textbf{RQ2---Fresh point-query system performance.}
Does the frozen system preserve verified success while reducing FEV, P95 latency,
fallback work, and computation on known-infeasible requests, with P50, P99, OOD, and
defer outcomes reported rather than omitted?

\noindent\textbf{RQ3---Fresh transition-rich trajectory performance.}
Relative to the \fixedhard, does \method\ preserve or improve whole-path completion
while reducing aggregate latency, FEV, and upper frame-latency quantiles under
method-specific closed-loop state?

\section{CG-HIK Method}

\subsection{Shared Numerical Solver Portfolio}

The three actions are entries into the same escalating cascade
(Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). The easy entry begins with one DLS update initialized at
$q_{t-1}$. If verification fails, it escalates to medium and then hard. Medium begins
with the best learned history-conditioned candidate and a bounded DLS refinement, then
escalates to hard. Hard runs longer DLS attempts from the learned candidate and
$q_{t-1}$ and, if needed, a bounded trust-region-reflective fallback from heterogeneous
previous, learned, and retrieved seed-bank configurations. Duplicated seeds are
removed. The \fixedrobust\ always enters at easy; the \fixedhard\ starts directly at
hard.

For a DLS step with task residual $e$ and Jacobian $J$, the update has the weighted,
damped form
\begin{equation}
  \Delta q = J_w^\top\left(J_wJ_w^\top+\lambda^2 I\right)^{-1}e_w,
\end{equation}
with adaptive damping, joint-step clipping, and joint-limit clipping. The frozen
portfolio uses the same tolerances, budgets, seed bank, fallback, and verifier for all
methods (Table~\ref{tab:configuration}). Entry names describe execution order, not
latent difficulty; an easy entry can still traverse the complete cascade after local
failure.

\subsection{History-Conditioned Candidate Proposal}

A \SeedEnsembleMembers{}-member feed-forward ensemble proposes bounded joint increments
conditioned on the previous configuration and desired pose. Each member contains
\SeedModelHiddenLayers\ hidden layers of width \SeedModelHiddenWidth\ and predicts a
normalized increment clipped to a maximum joint-space
step. The ensemble is not used as an acceptance mechanism. Its best candidate is
selected by a composite score over position and orientation residual, joint step,
and joint-limit penalty, then supplied to medium/hard numerical refinement.

The routing feature vector contains \RoutingFeatureCount\ diagnostics available after candidate
preparation: learned-candidate position and orientation residual, mean and maximum
ensemble uncertainty, candidate Jacobian minimum singular value, candidate
joint-limit margin, candidate-to-history joint step, and the position and orientation
step from the current end-effector pose. These features describe both the proposal and
the local query geometry. No feature relaxes the verifier.

\subsection{Action-Complete Pathway Data Collection}

For each development query, we execute easy, medium, and hard entries rather than
inferring a label from a heuristic category. Every action retains repeated,
Latin-rotated wall-clock measurements and the terminal verifier result, FEV,
iterations, fallback status, executed stages, and command. An additional fixed-cascade
audit arm verifies that entering at easy has identical semantics to the fixed primary
baseline. The resulting matrix is action-complete: all alternative pathways were
observed for the same query.

We occasionally use the term \emph{counterfactual} for these alternative action
records. It does not denote an estimate of unobserved potential outcomes. All actions
were actually run during development, so \emph{action-complete pathway supervision}
is the more precise description. Data roles are separated by query identity: model
fitting, probability/OOD calibration, and policy selection use disjoint development
subsets, and no fresh test outcome participates in training or threshold selection.

\subsection{Shared Success and Action-Specific Latency Prediction}

The predictor is a SiLU multilayer perceptron with \RoutingFeatureCount\ inputs and
\RoutingHiddenLayers\ hidden layers of width \RoutingHiddenWidth; its hidden
representation is shared by all heads. It has (i) one calibrated terminal-success logit broadcast across the three
entries, (ii) six positive outputs for entry-specific P50 and non-crossing P95
latency, and (iii) a calibrated fail-all logit. P50 and P95 are optimized with pinball
loss against all raw timing repeats, rather than against unstable single-run winners.
Platt scaling is fitted only on the calibration role
\cite{guo2017calibration}. The success head is shared because action-complete
development data showed exact terminal semantic invariance across entries under the
common fallback; separate action-success heads would imply differences not present in
the labels.

The latency heads serve a different purpose. Entry determines which prefix is skipped
and therefore changes cost even when the terminal command is the same. Predicting both
median and upper-tail pathway cost lets the selection objective prioritize
deadline-relevant P95. The labels exclude the router's own inference cost; the fresh
end-to-end measurements include it and therefore expose its fixed P50 overhead.

\subsection{Tail-Latency-Aware Routing Policy}

After calibration, an entry is eligible when the shared success probability is at
least the frozen minimum and its predicted P95 does not exceed the frozen
\SI{\RoutingDeadlineMilliseconds}{ms} deadline. The policy selects the eligible entry with minimum predicted
P95. If the predicted advantage over the most conservative eligible entry is smaller
than the frozen tie margin, it keeps the conservative entry. This prevents small,
noisy predicted differences from inducing an aggressive skip.

The policy, its threshold, the model weights, and all numerical budgets are frozen
before either fresh evaluation. The Categorical routing baseline and Cartesian-step
threshold baseline are retained only as development baselines: the former predicts
entry labels, whereas the latter routes from target displacement alone. Neither is
the final method.

\subsection{Reject, Conservative Defer, and Deterministic Verification}

The predictor also estimates the probability that the complete portfolio fails. A
query can be rejected only when no numerical entry is eligible, the fail-all
probability exceeds the frozen threshold, and the embedding is not flagged as OOD.
Reject executes zero numerical stages and returns no command. When the embedding is
OOD, or when no entry is eligible without high fail-all agreement, the policy defers
to the complete \fixedrobust. Thus an uncertain query receives more computation
rather than being declared infeasible.

For every numerical path, convergence is necessary but insufficient. The returned
configuration must pass Eq.~\eqref{eq:contract}; otherwise the runtime escalates or
returns failure. The learned system has no mechanism to override this decision. The
fresh trajectory evaluator additionally reruns the same contract independently on
every purportedly accepted command and counts any discrepancy as a contract
violation.

\subsection{Exact Batch-One Deployment}

The trained predictor, normalization, calibrators, OOD transform, and policy constants
are exported as a batch-one TorchScript module \cite{paszke2019pytorch}. Release
qualification compares eager and exported outputs, routing, command, FEV, fallback,
and verification reason under frozen tolerances. At evaluation time the seed proposal
runs on the GPU and the compact routing gate on the CPU, matching the sealed runtime
specification. This exact export matters because algorithmic savings can be erased by
per-query framework dispatch. It does not make the measurements hard real-time bounds;
it makes the evaluated batch-one implementation explicit and reproducible.

\section{Experimental Protocol}

\subsection{Systems, software, and frozen evidence roles}

We evaluate Franka Emika Panda and Universal Robots UR5e serial manipulators using
their exact URDF kinematics in Pinocchio \cite{carpentier2019pinocchio}. SciPy supplies the
trust-region-reflective fallback \cite{virtanen2020scipy}, and PyTorch supplies the
learned modules and exact export \cite{paszke2019pytorch}; scikit-learn utilities are
used in the development calibration stack \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}. The benchmark
does not step a physics simulator. All calls are batch one, CUDA synchronization is
included at method boundaries where applicable, and each comparator has an independent
runtime instance while sharing configuration semantics.

Development roles establish heterogeneity, train the model, calibrate probabilities
and OOD score, and select the routing policy. Fresh point-query and fresh trajectory
roles are immutable evaluation roles. Model weights, policy thresholds, solver
budgets, fallback, and verifier are loaded from a sealed release and are not refitted
or selected using evaluation outcomes.

\subsection{RQ1: action-complete development study}

The action-complete corpus contains \DevelopmentQueryCount\ queries across the two robots.
For every query, each of the three decision entries is executed with
\DevelopmentTimingRepeats\ interleaved timing repeats. Risk-training, calibration, and policy
selection identities are disjoint. We define the empirical oracle as the successful
entry with the lowest observed P95; if no entry succeeds, the oracle is reject.
Reported diagnostics are oracle-entry distribution, query-family by oracle-entry
rates, the latency/FEV gap from fixed entries to the oracle, policy--oracle agreement,
routing regret, and raw-sample quantile coverage. This stage diagnoses mechanism and
predictability; it is not confirmatory evidence.

\subsection{RQ2: fresh point-query evaluation}

For each robot, the primary frozen point analysis contains
\PointFeasibleQueriesPerRobot\ feasible queries---\PointIDFeasibleQueriesPerRobot\ in-distribution and
\PointOODFeasibleQueriesPerRobot\ shifted feasible points---plus
\PointInfeasibleQueriesPerRobot\ known-infeasible or one-frame-inadmissible points. Each
feasible point has \PointTimingRepeats\ raw solve calls per method. Training seed
\PointPrimaryTrainingSeed\ is the primary released system; seeds
\PointSensitivityTrainingSeeds\ are sensitivity runs on the same test
queries, not independent replications.

The primary comparator is the \fixedrobust, which shares candidate proposal,
numerical stages, fallback, and verifier and always enters at easy. Metrics include
verified success, FEV, fallback, P50/P95/P99, deadline misses, known-infeasible
rejection and avoided FEV. We also report Mahalanobis OOD AUROC/AUPRC and defer
recovery. The legacy frozen formal suite controlled the prespecified point and
trajectory-completion hypotheses jointly by Holm. RQ2 reports the
point outcomes; that legacy trajectory arm is distinct from the final transition-rich
evaluation in RQ3. Separate finite operational gates are reported without being
folded into the Holm family.

\subsection{RQ3: one-shot transition-rich trajectories}

The final evaluation contains \FreshTrajectoriesPerRobot\ new closed-loop trajectories
per robot, \FreshTrajectoriesPerFamily\ from each of four preregistered families:
smooth--fast-orientation--smooth, regular--near-singular--regular, central--joint-limit
skim--return, and slow--high-curvature/high-speed--slow. Every trajectory has
\FreshTrajectoryFrames\ frames at $\Delta t=\ControlIntervalMilliseconds$ ms. Identities were frozen
before solver calls and checked by query hash, trajectory UID, numeric ID, and seed
against registered development and prior evaluation lineages. Each method evolves its
own previous joint state; therefore paired frame comparisons share the exogenous
target and time index but not necessarily the complete solver input after branches
diverge.

Only three methods are executed: the \fixedrobust, the \fixedhard, and sealed
\method. Every method is called once per frame in a balanced Latin order. The
\fixedhard\ is the primary comparator because it was the strongest fixed entry in
development; the \fixedrobust\ shows performance relative to the original full
sequence. Whole-trajectory completion requires all \FreshTrajectoryFrames\ commands to be
verified. The frozen gate requires \method\ completion not below hard, aggregate
cumulative-latency and mean-FEV ratios no larger than
\TrajectoryCumulativeGateMaximum\ and \TrajectoryFEVGateMaximum, respectively; a P95
ratio no larger than \TrajectoryPNinetyFiveGateMaximum; a P99 ratio no larger than
\TrajectoryPNinetyNineGateMaximum; and zero contract violations. P50 is report-only.

\subsection{Statistical and timing conventions}

Point latency uses raw call-level quantiles because \PointTimingRepeats\ interleaved
solve calls are part of the frozen estimand. Development latency heads are trained on
\DevelopmentTimingRepeats\ raw repeats per entry. Trajectory latency is one retained
solve call per method and frame; frames
are temporally dependent, and the independent descriptive unit for completion is the
whole trajectory. Cumulative latency is the exact sum over all fixed-length frames,
and per-trajectory mean, median, and P95 are computed from
\FreshTrajectoriesPerRobot\ trajectory sums.
Bootstrap tests in the point study resample the locked independent point unit and use
the preregistered margins. We do not treat repeated calls or frames as independent
replicates.

\section{Results}

\subsection{RQ1: solver demand was heterogeneous and predictable}

No fixed entry dominated the empirical oracle. Among successful development queries,
Panda oracle assignments were \PandaDevOracleEasy{} easy,
\PandaDevOracleMedium{} medium, and \PandaDevOracleHard{} hard; UR5e assignments
were \URDevOracleEasy, \URDevOracleMedium, and
\URDevOracleHard, respectively. Hard was the best single fixed entry, yet its mean
excess over the oracle remained \PandaDevBestFixedOracleGap{} ms for Panda and
\URDevBestFixedOracleGap{} ms for UR5e. Starting at easy left larger mean gaps of
\PandaDevEasyOracleGap{} and \URDevEasyOracleGap{} ms, with the strongest
hard preference in the hard-valid family.

Among successful, non-abstained queries in the development policy-selection role,
\method\ selected the exact oracle entry for \PandaDevOracleAgreement{} on Panda and
\URDevOracleAgreement{} on UR5e. Exact agreement is a strict
criterion: an alternative entry can have practically indistinguishable cost.
Mean routing regret was only \PandaDevRoutingRegretMean\ and
\URDevRoutingRegretMean\ ms, with medians
\PandaDevRoutingRegretMedian/\URDevRoutingRegretMedian\ ms and P95
\PandaDevRoutingRegretPNinetyFive/\URDevRoutingRegretPNinetyFive\ ms.
Raw-sample P95 coverage was close to its nominal level across entries.
Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity} shows that the useful signal is a family- and
query-dependent cost ordering, not a universal easy/medium/hard taxonomy.

\subsection{Benefits concentrated in expensive trajectory families}

Family-level outcomes explain the aggregate savings (Figure~\ref{fig:family}). Among
families with at least one completed trajectory on both robots, the
regular--near-singular--regular family had the largest cumulative-latency reductions:
\PandaNearSingularCumReduction{} for Panda and
\URNearSingularCumReduction{} for UR5e. High-curvature/high-speed trajectories also
benefited (\PandaHighCurvatureCumReduction{} and
\URHighCurvatureCumReduction), and smooth--orientation transitions showed more
moderate reductions (\PandaSmoothCumReduction{} and
\URSmoothCumReduction). In contrast, the Panda joint-limit-skim reductions were
\PandaJointLimitCumReduction{} for cumulative latency and
\PandaJointLimitFEVReduction{} for FEV; the negative values indicate a slight
regression relative to hard. UR5e's larger nominal joint-limit reduction occurred
with zero completed trajectories under both methods and can reflect earlier failure,
so it is not evidence of lower cost on successful paths. The Panda exception is
important: when hard entry is already close to the effective
per-query choice, prediction overhead and occasional routing choices can offset the
saved work.

Panda's high-curvature/high-speed family remained difficult for every method, with only
\PandaHighCurvatureCGCompletion/\FreshTrajectoriesPerFamily\ complete \method\
trajectories. The near-singular family, by contrast, combines high recovery cost with
substantial entry heterogeneity, so skipping unproductive stages yields a larger
return. The representative trace in Figure~\ref{fig:family} shows how route changes
coincide with bursts of latency and FEV; it is illustrative and not a separate
statistical unit.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why query-adaptive computation works}

The action-complete corpus shows that numerical demand is heterogeneous even when the
portfolio, target contract, and terminal fallback are fixed. This gives the router a
real decision to make. The hard entry is the best global fixed action, but easy and
medium remain empirical winners for substantial subsets, while some families pay a
large penalty for beginning too shallowly. \method\ does not need perfect oracle
classification to help: many disagreements have small regret, and avoiding a few
expensive prefixes or fallbacks can dominate the aggregate total.

This also clarifies the role of learning. The model is not approximating the final IK
map in place of geometry. It predicts how an already available solver portfolio is
likely to behave. Because pathway outcomes were observed for the same development
queries, the target is closer to operational cost than a hand-labeled difficulty
class. The strongest evidence appears where failure recovery is expensive:
near-singular and high-curvature/high-speed trajectories. Smooth or hard-aligned
families leave less avoidable work.

\subsection{Why P50 rises while cumulative latency and P95 fall}

The router pays candidate-feature preparation and compact model inference on each
query. For the large mass of frames that the hard entry already solves cheaply, this
fixed cost raises the median by \PandaTrajectoryMedianIncrease{} for Panda and
\URTrajectoryMedianIncrease\ for UR5e in the final trajectory test. Tail
and cumulative metrics weight a different mechanism: difficult frames can trigger
many numerical evaluations and expensive fallback. Selecting a sufficient later entry
or rejecting a known-infeasible point avoids that work. Consequently, Panda and UR5e
show large cumulative and FEV reductions, lower P95, and, for UR5e, lower P99, even
though P50 rises. Panda P99 remains tied because a small set of hard numerical tails
survives routing. These observations are why the objective and claims emphasize
tail-aware resource allocation rather than universally faster queries.

\subsection{Learning is not the acceptance authority}

Every route ends at the same command contract. A high predicted success probability
cannot authorize a command, and a learned proposal cannot bypass numerical
refinement. The policy can decide to spend less work, more work, or no work; only the
verifier can turn a numerical result into an accepted command. This separation makes
failure observable and keeps the primary comparison interpretable. It also explains
why zero contract violations are more informative than raw solver convergence: they
show consistency with the implemented kinematic contract, not with an unspecified
notion of safety.

\subsection{Alternative temporal shortcuts}

Development-only mechanism studies tested lighter per-frame gates and stateful local
shortcuts. They confirmed that one-step local solving can produce long successful
runs and very low median latency. They also revealed a structural risk: maintaining a
locally verified branch changes subsequent $q_{t-1}$, and a later near-singular or
transition-rich request may then become unrecoverable even when a hard-only history
would succeed. Sparse hard re-anchoring reduced neither all long robust episodes nor
all robot-specific completion losses under the preregistered gates. These studies are
not formal results and introduce no component of the final method. They motivate
retaining per-query pathway evaluation instead of committing to a temporal shortcut.

\section{Limitations}

First, the evidence uses exact URDF/Pinocchio forward kinematics and Jacobians. It is
not a dynamics, physics-simulation, or physical-robot experiment. Robot assets were
drawn from an Isaac Sim installation, but Isaac Lab dynamics were not executed.
Collision, self-collision, contact, torque limits, actuator dynamics, sensor delay,
and low-level controller tracking are outside the contract and must be evaluated
before deployment.

Second, Eq.~\eqref{eq:contract} is not a universal safety proof. It checks finite joint
commands, pose residual, URDF joint bounds, and one-step velocity continuity only.
Acceleration, jerk, swept-volume collision, force, and task-specific constraints are
absent. Verified throughout the paper means verified against this exact kinematic
contract.

Third, the Mahalanobis embedding score is a conservative switch, not a strong OOD
detector. Point-level AUROC was \PandaOODAUROC\ for Panda and \UROODAUROC\ for UR5e,
and no deferred point was recovered. OOD is therefore not a contribution supported by
the present evidence.

Fourth, latency is specific to the recorded software stack and hardware, with
batch-one execution and explicit synchronization. Percentiles are empirical
measurements, not hard real-time upper bounds. Fixed model inference raises P50, and
rare numerical fallback still dominates extreme Panda tails. Hardware, compiler, and
runtime changes require new measurement.

Fifth, the fresh transition set is deliberately stress-rich rather than a prevalence
estimate for a deployment distribution. Panda high-curvature/high-speed completion
remains very low, so lower cost must not be read as solving that regime. Closed-loop
methods can follow different joint branches after an early command difference; their
trajectory comparisons are policy-level evaluations under common exogenous targets,
not identical-query microbenchmarks at every later frame.

Finally, models are robot-specific and evaluated on two manipulators. The study does
not establish cross-robot transfer, collision-aware admissibility, or generalization
to unseen hardware. Extending the contract and evaluating controller-level execution
are separate next steps; they should preserve the current separation between learned
allocation, numerical command generation, and deterministic acceptance.

\section{Conclusions}

\method\ treats online inverse kinematics as query-adaptive allocation of a shared
numerical solver portfolio. Action-complete development observations make pathway
cost and terminal success learnable without inventing difficulty labels. A compact
tail-latency policy chooses among nested entries, distinguishes confident rejection
from conservative defer, and leaves every joint command to numerical geometry and a
common deterministic verifier.

The two frozen evaluations give a coherent but qualified result. On fresh point
queries, \method\ preserved verified feasible success, reduced FEV and P95, and
avoided most numerical work on known-infeasible requests; P50 increased, Panda P99
barely changed, point-OOD discrimination was weak, and defer showed no recovery. On
the final transition-rich trajectories, completion matched or exceeded the strongest
fixed entry, cumulative time fell by \PandaTrajectoryCumulativeReduction{} and
\URTrajectoryCumulativeReduction, mean FEV fell by
\PandaTrajectoryFEVReduction{} and \URTrajectoryFEVReduction, and the frozen final
gate passed on both robots with zero contract violations. The improvement was largest
where query demand and recovery cost were heterogeneous, not in every family or every
latency quantile. The central conclusion is therefore precise:
\emph{learning allocates solver effort per query; numerical geometry generates joint
commands; deterministic verification governs acceptance.}

\appendix
\section{Supplementary Evidence Boundaries}

\subsection{Point-test multiplicity and operational gate}

The primary point system uses training seed \PointPrimaryTrainingSeed. Two additional
training seeds (\PointSensitivityTrainingSeeds) reuse
the same robot-specific test queries and therefore quantify sensitivity rather than
independent replication. Prespecified point margins and a legacy
trajectory-completion margin per robot form one joint Holm family. All margin nulls
were rejected.
The legacy trajectory arm is distinct from the final transition-rich evaluation. The
separate operational gate included a
strictly positive OOD-feasible false-reject improvement; Panda fixed and \method\ both
had zero false rejects, so that condition and the point-study paper gate were false.
No threshold or claim boundary was changed after observing this result.

\subsection{Development-only baselines and mechanism studies}

The Categorical routing baseline and Cartesian-step threshold baseline are
development comparisons, not the final method. Additional lightweight and temporal
shortcut studies are retained in the repository as development-only mechanism
analyses. They are excluded from the fresh final trajectory comparison and from the
three main result tables.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The evidence builder reads frozen JSON/NPZ files, validates their recorded hashes,
and emits the TeX macros and CSV files used by the tables and figures. The figure
builder reads only those CSV files. Rebuilding the paper therefore does not execute an
IK query, load a test solver, retrain a model, or alter an evaluation output.
