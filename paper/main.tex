\begin{abstract}
Online inverse kinematics (IK) must generate valid joint commands for queries with
different numerical demands. Fixed cascades already stop after an early success,
but their entry point can still impose unnecessary refinement prefixes or spend
large budgets on unsuccessful requests. We investigate the hypothesis that robust
coverage need not require uniform worst-case computation. CG-HIK learns
query-conditioned pathway costs from action-complete observations of a shared
solver portfolio and selects an entry using predicted tail latency. Learning
allocates solver effort; numerical geometry generates joint commands; deterministic
verification governs acceptance.

We evaluate the frozen method through the original held-out tests and a supplementary
mechanism study on Panda and UR5e. On 2,500 witness-feasible point queries per robot,
CG-HIK matched always-hard success but increased mean end-to-end time by 3.33\% and
29.32\%; a geometry rule and a reject-only control performed similarly. Selecting by
predicted P95 rather than P50 reduced FEV by 0.58\% and 1.86\%, without a resolved
P95 or P99 advantage. Reanalysis showed that 99.07\% and 101.31\% of the original
trajectory time savings lay in trajectories neither method completed, offsetting
additional cost on jointly completed paths. On new continuously feasible reference
paths, CG-HIK completed 34/40 versus 37/40 for always-hard on Panda and 36/40 for both
on UR5e. A Panda joint-limit family retained successful-solve savings, whereas
near-singular branch evolution caused three lost completions. Official TRAC-IK
provided a substantially faster external comparison. These findings identify
failure handling as the dominant aggregate benefit in the tested portfolio and
show why learned entry selection, successful-path computation, and acceptance
validity require separate evidence.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} inverse kinematics; adaptive computation; algorithm
selection; tail latency; damped least squares; hybrid learning and optimization;

\section{Introduction}

Online inverse kinematics (IK) repeatedly converts end-effector targets into joint
commands. A request consists of a desired pose, the previous accepted configuration,
and a finite command interval. The returned command changes the state from which the
next request is solved, making branch continuity and recovery part of the problem
rather than optional post-processing
\cite{rakita2018relaxedik,morgan2024cppflow,nguyen2026sequential,yang2026mimik}.
Nearby targets may need different amounts of work because the current configuration
can approach a singularity, a joint limit, or an unfavorable inverse branch.
Online IK therefore couples query-level computational cost to the trajectory of
accepted commands.

Analytical and numerical IK supply complementary tools for this interface.
Structural decompositions can enumerate branches rapidly for compatible geometries
\cite{elias2025ikgeo,ostermeier2025eaik,lopezcustodio2025geofik}.
Jacobian and damped-least-squares (DLS) methods exploit a good local initialization,
with damping limiting amplification near rank loss
\cite{wampler1986dls,nakamura1986singularity,chiaverini1994review}.
Restarts, constrained optimization, and complementary solver portfolios broaden
recovery \cite{beeson2015tracik,colan2024variablestep,wu2024ikspark,zhang2026viability,yasutake2026hjcdik}.
Their common strength is explicit geometry: a proposed configuration can be checked
against pose, joint, and single-step continuity conditions before acceptance.
The availability of several useful stages, however, leaves their scheduling open.

Learned and generative IK improve a different part of the pipeline: finding useful
candidates in a set-valued inverse map \cite{siciliano1990tutorial}.
Conditional flows, diffusion, graph representations, invertible networks, and set
predictors represent alternative solutions or redundancy
\cite{ames2022ikflow,limoyo2025ggik,diprasetya2025kinenn,go2025emiknet,zhang2025ikdiffuser,nguyen2026setik}.
History conditioning can favor the neighborhood of a previous configuration
\cite{demby2024learning,nguyen2026sequential,yang2026mimik}.
These models amortize proposal generation, but candidate quality and verified
system cost are different quantities. Similar proposal errors can lead to different
refinement work, and a neural output can still violate a command constraint.

Hybrid proposal--refinement systems combine these strengths. Learned candidates
supply an initialization; numerical geometry restores precision; explicit checks
govern the returned command. IKFlow and CycleIK support numerical refinement
\cite{ames2022ikflow,habekost2023cycleik}, while path-level generative proposals can
be coupled to optimization and checking \cite{morgan2024cppflow}.
Diffusion-seeded, execution-aware, graph, and neuro-fuzzy approaches offer further
forms of this cooperation
\cite{huang2025diffusionseeder,carvalho2025mpd,tang2025etaik,jayabalan2026hybrid,jlidi2026xgnn}.
Once several refinement paths coexist, a hybrid system also has to decide how much
of that available computation to use for the current query.

A fixed cascade already implements conditional computation: it stops on verified
success and escalates on failure. Its remaining limitation is the fixed \emph{entry},
not execution of every stage on every request. An inexpensive prefix can either
solve a continuation or add work before a later stage that was sufficient on its own.
Requests with little prospect of success can consume a large fallback budget.
Seed selection and failure detection address related decisions
\cite{yuan2025iksel,xu2025faildetect}, but do not by themselves establish that learning
complete pathway costs improves on a strong fixed entry or a simple geometric rule.
The allocation question must include prediction overhead and distinguish savings
during successful solving from savings in failure handling.

We study this question with \method, a query-conditioned allocation policy over one
shared numerical portfolio, following the per-instance algorithm-selection viewpoint
\cite{rice1976selection,bischl2016aslib}. The hypothesis is that
\emph{robust coverage does not require uniform worst-case computation}.
Executing every entry on the same development queries replaces manual difficulty
labels with observed success and cost. The policy predicts pathway latency, selects
a sufficient entry, and can decline a request predicted to exhaust the portfolio.
Its responsibilities remain separate:
\emph{learning allocates solver effort per query; numerical geometry generates joint
commands; deterministic verification governs acceptance}.
The frozen implementation still prepares learned candidates on every query; its
allocation decision concerns subsequent numerical work, not elimination of all
neural inference on easy requests.

Our contributions are the shared-contract allocation formulation, action-complete
pathway supervision, and an empirical separation of entry selection, rejection,
prediction overhead, and closed-loop effects. The original held-out tests compare
the method with a fixed early-entry cascade. A supplementary study then asks the
stronger questions that this comparison leaves open: whether the learned entry
beats hard entry or a simple rule, whether P95 selection adds measurable value,
whether savings persist on continuously feasible paths, and where the system sits
relative to an external numerical solver. The resulting evidence includes local
successful-solve gains and substantial failure-handling savings, but does not support
a general learned-routing advantage over always-hard. This distinction makes the
resource-allocation hypothesis testable rather than assuming that a lower aggregate
runtime identifies its mechanism.

\section{Related Work}

The computation-allocation problem lies at the intersection of four established
lines of work: numerical IK, learned multi-solution proposal, hybrid refinement, and
per-instance solver selection. Their complementary strengths define both the building
blocks of \method\ and the allocation question that remains.

\subsection{Numerical, Analytical, and Constrained IK}

Classical IK spans exact structural solutions and general iterative updates.
Geometric and analytical methods exploit screw structure and subproblem
decomposition, providing rapid branch enumeration for suitable manipulators
\cite{elias2025ikgeo,ostermeier2025eaik,lopezcustodio2025geofik}. Jacobian inverse and
pseudoinverse methods generalize across chains, while DLS and singularity-robust
inverses stabilize updates near rank loss
\cite{wampler1986dls,nakamura1986singularity,chiaverini1994review}. Modern numerical
extensions vary the step, run complementary solvers, batch initializations, or encode
viability constraints
\cite{beeson2015tracik,colan2024variablestep,wu2024ikspark,zhang2026viability,yasutake2026hjcdik}.
RelaxedIK shows how pose, continuity, collision, and singularity objectives can be
treated jointly as online motion synthesis \cite{rakita2018relaxedik}, while recent
analysis distinguishes local numerical conditioning from the topology of branch
transitions \cite{boschi2026singularity}. These methods make robust IK a portfolio of
complementary local and recovery mechanisms. \method\ adds a learned allocation
policy over that fixed portfolio.

\subsection{Learned Multi-Solution and History-Conditioned IK}

Learned IK has progressed from point regression toward explicit representations of a
set-valued inverse. Conditional normalizing flows, graph-equivariant generators,
invertible modular networks, multiple-instance models, diffusion, and
permutation-invariant set prediction improve diversity or structural transfer
\cite{ames2022ikflow,limoyo2025ggik,diprasetya2025kinenn,go2025emiknet,zhang2025ikdiffuser,nguyen2026setik}.
History-conditioned models add a reference configuration or predict a joint increment,
making branch preference explicit rather than averaging globally separated solutions
\cite{demby2024learning,nguyen2026sequential,yang2026mimik}. The diffusion and MimicIK
records are treated here as preprints, and the sequential model as an online
forthcoming record. Collectively, these methods show the value of learned proposal
distributions. \method\ uses such a proposal upstream, but evaluates it through
downstream numerical pathways and never treats a neural prediction as an accepted
command.

\subsection{Learned Proposals with Numerical Refinement}

Hybrid pipelines combine the global coverage of learning with the precision and
constraint handling of numerical geometry. IKFlow and CycleIK refine learned
solutions numerically \cite{ames2022ikflow,habekost2023cycleik}; CppFlow performs
path-level proposal, search, and refinement \cite{morgan2024cppflow}; and diffusion
priors can seed or guide trajectory optimization
\cite{huang2025diffusionseeder,carvalho2025mpd}. Execution-time-aware redundancy
selection, graph warm starts, and neuro-fuzzy approximators likewise make learned
outputs useful through their effect on a downstream solver
\cite{tang2025etaik,jayabalan2026hybrid,jlidi2026xgnn}. We retain this cooperative
architecture but shift the learned target from the joint command to the amount and
entry of numerical work. This enables an interpretable system comparison: the primary
baselines and \method\ share proposals, solvers, fallback, budgets, and verification,
differing in their computation-allocation rule.

\subsection{Adaptive Computation and Solver Routing}

Per-instance algorithm selection maps instance features to algorithms with different
cost and success profiles \cite{rice1976selection}. Benchmark libraries formalize the
corresponding instance--algorithm performance matrix and the distinction between a
selected system and its empirical oracle \cite{bischl2016aslib}. Calibration and
ensembles provide tools for controlling predictive confidence
\cite{guo2017calibration,lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, while runtime failure
detection motivates conservative fallback under uncertainty \cite{xu2025faildetect}.
Within IK, seed selection and reselection already demonstrate that basin choice and
failure recovery affect runtime \cite{yuan2025iksel}. \method\ applies per-instance
algorithm selection to the entries of a shared IK portfolio, using observed terminal
success and query-conditioned P95 cost as routing targets. This places learning at the
computation-allocation layer while retaining the proposal, numerical refinement, and
verification mechanisms established by prior work. We formalize that decision next.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Online query and command contract}

At control step $t$, an IK query is
\begin{equation}
  \xi_t = \left(x_t^d, q_{t-1}, \Delta t\right),
\end{equation}
where $x_t^d=(p_t^d,R_t^d)\in SE(3)$ is the desired end-effector pose,
$q_{t-1}\in\mathcal{Q}$ is the previously accepted configuration, and
$\Delta t$ is the command interval. A numerical pathway proposes $q_t$. The shared
deterministic contract is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\contract(q_t;\xi_t)=
&\ \mathbf{1}\!\left[\|p(q_t)-p_t^d\|_2 \le \varepsilon_p\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[d_R(R(q_t),R_t^d)\le\varepsilon_R\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[q_{\min}-\epsilon_q \le q_t\le q_{\max}+\epsilon_q\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[|\Delta q_t|\le \dot q_{\max}\Delta t+\epsilon_v\right]\\
&\land \mathbf{1}\!\left[q_t\text{ is finite}\right].
\end{split}
\label{eq:contract}
\end{equation}
The orientation distance $d_R$ is the norm of the rotation logarithm. This is a
kinematic command contract, not a universal safety certificate: it contains no
collision, torque, contact, or tracking model.

\subsection{Entry actions and tail-cost objective}

Let $\mathcal{A}=\{\mathrm{easy},\mathrm{medium},\mathrm{hard}\}$ denote entry actions
into a nested numerical cascade. For query $\xi$ and entry $a$, let
$Y(\xi,a)\in\{0,1\}$ be terminal verified success and let $T(\xi,a)$ be the observed
action-pathway wall-clock latency, including proposal preparation, numerical solving,
verification, and pathway framework overhead. The learned routing decision itself is
excluded from these development labels because each action is forced during
collection. Repeated measurements define conditional latency quantiles
$Q_\tau(T\mid\xi,a)$. Fresh system measurements instead enclose the complete runtime,
including the router's fixed batch-one overhead.

The learned policy predicts $\widehat p_s(\xi)$ and entry-specific
$\widehat Q_{0.50}(\xi,a)$ and $\widehat Q_{0.95}(\xi,a)$. Given a calibrated success
threshold $\pi_{\min}$ and deadline $D$, the eligible set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}(\xi)=
  \left\{a\in\mathcal{A}:
  \widehat p_s(\xi)\ge\pi_{\min},\
  \widehat Q_{0.95}(\xi,a)\le D\right\}.
\end{equation}
When this set is nonempty, the idealized routing rule is
\begin{equation}
  a^\star(\xi)=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}(\xi)}
  \widehat Q_{0.95}(\xi,a),
\label{eq:route}
\end{equation}
with a conservative tie rule described below. Empty eligibility does not itself imply
infeasibility: the policy must distinguish confident portfolio failure from
uncertainty.

\subsection{Research questions}

The evaluation links three questions.

\noindent\textbf{RQ1---Allocation opportunity.}
How large and stable are pathway-cost differences when fixed entries, the learned
policy, and an empirical oracle are compared on the same queries?

\noindent\textbf{RQ2---Entry choice, rejection, and tail-aware selection.}
On identical fresh queries, does learned entry choice outperform hard entry and a
simple rule, and does P95-based selection improve on P50-based selection once actual
runtime overhead is included?

\noindent\textbf{RQ3---Successful continuous computation.}
Do savings persist while completing continuously feasible trajectories, how much
comes from handling failures, and how does performance compare with TRAC-IK?

The method defines the allocation and acceptance decisions first. The experiments
then separate these mechanisms with fixed-query controls and method-specific
closed-loop state.

\section{CG-HIK Method}

\method\ separates the choice of computational effort from the generation and
acceptance of a joint command. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} follows that separation from
the shared numerical portfolio, through pathway observations and tail-cost routing,
to deterministic verification.

\subsection{Shared Numerical Solver Portfolio}

An early-terminating cascade can still pay for an unnecessary prefix before reaching
a sufficient stage. We therefore treat the three actions as alternative entries
into the same escalating cascade (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). The easy
entry begins with one DLS update initialized at
$q_{t-1}$. If verification fails, it escalates to medium and then hard. Medium begins
with the best learned history-conditioned candidate and a bounded DLS refinement, then
escalates to hard. Hard runs longer DLS attempts from the learned candidate and
$q_{t-1}$ and, if needed, a bounded trust-region-reflective fallback from heterogeneous
previous, learned, and retrieved seed-bank configurations. Duplicated seeds are
removed. The \fixedrobust\ always enters at easy; the \fixedhard\ starts directly at
hard.

For a DLS step with task residual $e$ and Jacobian $J$, the update has the weighted,
damped form
\begin{equation}
  \Delta q = J_w^\top\left(J_wJ_w^\top+\lambda^2 I\right)^{-1}e_w,
\end{equation}
with adaptive damping, joint-step clipping, and joint-limit clipping. The shared
portfolio uses the same tolerances, budgets, seed bank, fallback, and verifier for all
methods (Table~\ref{tab:configuration}). Entry names describe execution order, not
latent difficulty; an easy entry can still traverse the complete cascade after local
failure.

\subsection{History-Conditioned Candidate Proposal}

Previous-state continuation is inexpensive in a favorable basin but can be a poor
initializer after a difficult target or branch transition. The hard pathways therefore
need a stronger proposal without allowing that proposal to become a command source. A
\SeedEnsembleMembers{}-member feed-forward ensemble proposes bounded joint increments
conditioned on the previous configuration and desired pose. Each member contains
\SeedModelHiddenLayers\ hidden layers of width \SeedModelHiddenWidth\ and predicts a
normalized increment clipped to a maximum joint-space
step. The ensemble is not used as an acceptance mechanism. Its best candidate is
selected by a composite score over position and orientation residual, joint step,
and joint-limit penalty, then supplied to medium/hard numerical refinement.

The frozen implementation prepares these learned candidates on every query,
including easy-entry and rejected queries. Routing can skip numerical prefixes;
it does not remove this proposal-generation cost. The direct hard baseline retains
the same proposals but omits diagnostic features and predictions it does not need.

The routing feature vector contains \RoutingFeatureCount\ diagnostics available after candidate
preparation: learned-candidate position and orientation residual, mean and maximum
ensemble uncertainty, candidate Jacobian minimum singular value, candidate
joint-limit margin, candidate-to-history joint step, and the position and orientation
step from the current end-effector pose. These features describe both the proposal and
the local query geometry. No feature relaxes the verifier.

\subsection{Action-Complete Pathway Data Collection}

Hand-assigned easy or hard labels cannot identify the least costly sufficient entry
once escalation, verification, and fallback are included. For each development query,
we therefore execute easy, medium, and hard entries rather than infer a label from a
heuristic category. Every action retains repeated,
Latin-rotated wall-clock measurements and the terminal verifier result, FEV,
iterations, fallback status, executed stages, and command. A fixed-cascade
control arm confirms that entering at easy reproduces the semantics of the primary
baseline. The resulting matrix is action-complete: all alternative pathways were
observed for the same query.

Because every entry is observed rather than imputed, we refer to this target as
\emph{action-complete pathway supervision}. Data roles are separated by query identity: model
fitting, probability/OOD calibration, and policy selection use disjoint development
subsets, and no fresh test outcome participates in training or threshold selection.

\subsection{Shared Success and Action-Specific Latency Prediction}

All entries eventually share the same terminal fallback and verifier. The
action-complete observations showed exact terminal semantic invariance across entries,
so separate action-success predictions would introduce distinctions absent from the
labels. This is an observed development regularity, not a guarantee for arbitrary
queries or for later states reached by different closed-loop methods. Entry choice
nevertheless changes which prefixes are executed and therefore
changes cost, even when the final command is the same. Rare failed prefixes and
fallback calls create the latency tail, which motivates predicting P95 as well as the
median.

The predictor is a SiLU multilayer perceptron with \RoutingFeatureCount\ inputs and
\RoutingHiddenLayers\ hidden layers of width \RoutingHiddenWidth; its hidden
representation is shared by all heads. It has (i) one calibrated terminal-success
logit broadcast across the three entries, (ii) six positive outputs for entry-specific
P50 and non-crossing P95 latency, and (iii) a calibrated fail-all logit. P50 and P95
are optimized with pinball loss against all raw timing repeats rather than unstable
single-run winners. Platt scaling is fitted only on the calibration role
\cite{guo2017calibration}. Development latency labels exclude the router's own
inference cost because every action is forced during collection; the fresh end-to-end
measurements include that cost and expose its fixed P50 overhead.

\subsection{Tail-Latency-Aware Routing Policy}

Low predicted latency is useful only when the selected pathway is also likely to be
sufficient. After calibration, an entry is therefore eligible when the shared success
probability is at least the prespecified minimum and its predicted P95 does not exceed the
\SI{\RoutingDeadlineMilliseconds}{ms} deadline. The policy selects the eligible entry with minimum predicted
P95. If the predicted advantage over the most conservative eligible entry is smaller
than the prespecified tie margin, it keeps the conservative entry. This prevents small,
noisy predicted differences from inducing an aggressive skip.

The policy, thresholds, model weights, and numerical budgets remain fixed in all
original and supplementary evaluations. The supplementary geometry and P50-selection
controls change the specified decision rule only; they are not new trained methods.

\subsection{Deterministic Verification, Reject, and Conservative Defer}

Predicted pathway cost or success is not proof that a joint command is valid. For
every numerical path, reported convergence alone is insufficient: the returned
configuration must pass Eq.~\eqref{eq:contract}, or the runtime escalates or reports
failure. The learned system cannot override that decision. The fresh trajectory
evaluator additionally reruns the same contract independently on every reported
accepted command and counts any discrepancy as a contract violation.

Some queries also make every available pathway predictably futile. The predictor
therefore estimates the probability that the complete portfolio fails. A query can be
rejected only when no numerical entry is eligible, the fail-all probability exceeds
the fixed threshold, and the embedding is not flagged as OOD. Reject executes zero
numerical stages and returns no command. Uncertainty is treated differently from
predicted futility: an OOD query, or one lacking sufficient fail-all agreement, is
deferred to the complete \fixedrobust. This defer outcome is an auxiliary conservative
mechanism rather than the main route by which \method\ saves computation.

\subsection{Batch-One Cost Accounting}

Per-query routing is worthwhile only if its implementation cost does not erase the
numerical work it avoids. To preserve batch-one cost accounting, the predictor and
policy are exported as one runtime module. The fresh evaluations include this routing
cost, so measured savings reflect the deployed execution path rather than solver work
alone.

\section{Experimental Protocol}

The original tests establish performance relative to the initial fixed cascade.
The supplementary evaluation probes the mechanism with stronger fixed-entry
controls, explicit feasibility witnesses, and an external solver. No supplementary
outcome is used to retrain the model or select its thresholds, budgets, or fallback.

\subsection{Shared implementation and timing}

Panda and UR5e use the repository's URDFKinematics forward-kinematics and geometric
Jacobian implementation, without a physics simulator. SciPy supplies the bounded
trust-region-reflective fallback \cite{virtanen2020scipy}; PyTorch supplies the
learned modules \cite{paszke2019pytorch}, and scikit-learn utilities support the
original calibration stack \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}. The supplementary measurements
use an Intel Core i7-13700K, an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000 for candidate generation,
PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128, eight intra-op threads and one inter-op thread. Methods are
measured sequentially, with no concurrent benchmark.

Whole-call timing includes input conversion, required candidate preparation,
diagnostics, prediction, decision, numerical solving, fallback, and in-runtime
verification. GPU synchronization encloses applicable calls. Initialization, warmup,
serialization, and independent verification replay are outside the timed interval.
The supplementary always-hard wrapper omits unused routing features and prediction
while retaining the frozen hard-stage commands and budgets; smoke comparisons
confirmed command, FEV, stage, and fallback agreement with the original wrapper.
Historical pathway-label timings and the original profiling wrapper are therefore
not substituted for this directly measured deployment baseline.

\subsection{Pathway observations and original result decomposition}

The original action-complete corpus contains \DevelopmentQueryCount\ queries, each
with \DevelopmentTimingRepeats\ interleaved calls per entry. Fitting, calibration,
and policy-selection roles are disjoint. To compare fixed easy, medium, hard, the
frozen policy, and an empirical oracle on one population, we use only successful,
non-abstained policy-validation queries: 2,031 for Panda and 2,049 for UR5e.
We report numerical-plus-verification cost separately from the historical label
pipeline, which also contains candidate preparation and a constant-risk profiling
shell. Deployment routing overhead was not measured on that population and is
left unreported. An oracle chosen from five-repeat empirical P95 is a noisy
in-sample diagnostic, not a known true optimum.

We also partition every original fresh trajectory by the two observed completion
outcomes: both complete, only \method\ completes, only always-hard completes, and
neither completes. All 150 frames remain in each trajectory's latency and FEV sum.
Group counts, costs, first failures, and reject/defer/fallback events add back to
the original totals. This is explicitly post-hoc descriptive decomposition;
outcome-conditioned groups are not causal treatment strata.

\subsection{Identical-query mechanism controls}

Each robot receives 3,000 newly generated point queries: 1,000 local continuations,
500 near-singular, 500 near-limit, 500 difficult feasible, and 500 constructively
inexecutable requests. The first 2,500 retain a reference joint configuration that
passes the unchanged verifier. Near-singular configurations are selected by geometric
Jacobian conditioning; feasible targets are generated by FK from bounded joint
increments. Inexecutable target positions lie outside a global reach bound obtained
from the sum of URDF segment-translation lengths for these fixed/revolute chains.
No query is labeled infeasible because an evaluated method fails.

All methods receive identical target pose, previous configuration, and interval.
The previous state is not updated between point calls. Each query has three
randomized, cyclically interleaved repetitions of the six internal controls in
Table~\ref{tab:controls}, plus all four TRAC-IK budgets. Geometry thresholds are
selected using old calibration labels only: 0.02 m/0.01 rad for Panda and
0.005 m/0.025 rad for UR5e. The geometry control shares the frozen reject/defer
predictor; it tests the need for learned \emph{entry ordering}, not the elimination
of all model cost.

A prespecified 500-query feasible subset per robot contains 200 local and 100 from
each other feasible family. Each forced entry is measured ten times. Five even
interleaved repeats select the lowest empirical P95 among entries successful on all
five; the five odd repeats evaluate that choice, and then the roles swap. Queries
without a successful selection entry are reported separately. We measure preference
stability, held-out cost relative to hard, and the fraction whose entry means differ
by at most 0.15 ms. The last threshold is a descriptive near-equivalence diagnostic,
not a trained parameter. No oracle choice feeds back into the method.

\subsection{External solver under the same command contract}

The external comparison uses official \texttt{trac\_ik\_lib} 2.2.0
\cite{beeson2015tracik} in Speed mode.\footnote{Upstream source commit:
\texttt{90162ac2ecc6ea8f88c6e99df6ee01efd217a3fb}.}
Its KDL and nonlinear optimization workers run concurrently. The unmodified official
sources are compiled with GNU C++ 9.5.0 and NLopt 2.7.1 behind a small C interface.
The adapter uses the same URDF, base/tool chain, joint ordering, and previous-state
initialization. KDL FK agrees with the public model below $7\times10^{-16}$ on
100 configurations per robot.

For every query, \texttt{setKDLLimits} intersects URDF bounds with the allowed
one-frame joint interval. Both supplied chains have bounded revolute active joints,
including UR5e's wide but finite bounds; they are not wrapped as continuous joints.
The adapter separately supports genuine continuous variables using a local
unwrapped search interval. Internal epsilon is $10^{-5}$ per Cartesian component
with zero additional Twist tolerance, stricter than the public pose tolerances.
A nonnegative solver return still requires the public verifier. Per-query bound
updates and their internal solver reconstruction are included in timing; model
loading is not. TRAC-IK FEV is unavailable under the internal definition and is
not compared.

All point results retain the fixed 5/20/100/400 ms search budgets. Curves count a
success only when a verified command actually returns within the displayed elapsed
time. The trajectory budget is selected independently on 120 old calibration
queries per robot, maximizing success, then minimizing measured total time, then
budget; both robots select 5 ms before point-test outcomes are opened.

\subsection{Continuously feasible reference trajectories}

Each robot has 40 new trajectories, ten each from smooth motion, a low-manipulability
neighborhood, joint-limit approach and return, and high-curvature/faster motion.
Every trajectory has 150 target frames at $\Delta t=0.02$ s. A reference joint path
is generated first and converted to targets through FK. Geometric amplitude scaling
enforces joint and one-step velocity constraints before evaluation; every reference
transition passes the public verifier. Seeds and trajectory identities are new and
fixed before comparison, with query-hash isolation recorded in the manifest.
No method's success rate is used to retain or reject a path.

Always-hard, routing-only, \method, and calibration-selected TRAC-IK start at the
same reference initial configuration. Online calls receive no future reference
joints, extra reference seed, or branch label. Each method then evolves its own
state: acceptance updates the state, failure holds it, and targets advance through
all 150 fixed indices. Reference feasibility proves the existence of a path, not
recoverability from every state an online method might reach.

Geometric completion requires acceptance at every frame. We separately report the
fraction of calls accepted within their measured 20 ms interval and the number of
trajectories accepted within 20 ms on every frame. A geometrically accepted late
command still updates state in this kinematic sequence evaluation, but does not
count as timely acceptance. Thus nominal $\Delta t$ does not establish real-time
control at 50 Hz. Lost and gained completion UIDs are reported, not suppressed
by requiring identical completion vectors.

\subsection{Statistical units and preservation}

Point uncertainty uses paired queries, retaining all three technical repetitions
within each resampled query. Trajectory uncertainty uses complete paired
trajectories, never 150 independent frames. We report ratios of total cost and
success differences with 95\% percentile intervals from 4,000 paired,
family-stratified bootstrap resamples. Frame quantiles are empirical whole-call
quantiles; paired quantile intervals resample query clusters. These are descriptive
intervals, without multiplicity-adjusted significance claims or a composite gate.

The original results and source data are unchanged. A first incomplete Panda
supplementary point run was interrupted after code inspection found a redundant
P95 sorting operation in the control adapter. That partial run was excluded in
full and preserved separately; no aggregate outcomes were inspected or used.
The corrected controls perform one inference and only the ranking required by
their definition. Fresh identities, calibration choices, the main method, and
numerical settings remained fixed. The complete supplementary measurements and
findings report precede this manuscript revision.

\section{Discussion}

The supplementary evidence separates three questions often compressed into one
runtime comparison: whether an entry avoids numerical work, whether its decision
overhead is repaid, and whether accepted commands preserve later recoverability.
The original aggregate gains are real under their recorded protocols, but their
dominant source differs from the stronger successful-routing interpretation.

\subsection{Allocation opportunity is not the same as learned advantage}

Action-complete data are useful because they expose the cost of every available
entry under a common command contract. They also expose when the allocation problem
offers little headroom. Fixed hard is close to the empirical oracle on the matched
population, and cross-fitting does not reveal a stable average improvement beyond it.
A low router regret can therefore arise from near-equivalent options rather than
accurate identification of a uniquely cheaper path.

The direct controls reinforce this reading. On feasible point inputs, full,
geometry, and reject-only variants have similar cost, and all pay more than direct
hard. The fixed cascade's existing early stopping already makes numerical work
query dependent; learned allocation must improve on that conditional execution,
not receive credit for it. P95 selection produces a small FEV benefit over P50
selection, but the measured tail intervals do not establish its necessity.
Predicting a tail cost remains a reasonable design objective whose incremental
benefit must be demonstrated, rather than inferred from the objective itself.

\subsection{Failure handling can dominate aggregate savings}

Rejecting a request likely to exhaust the portfolio can avoid much more work than
skipping a short numerical prefix. The old trajectory decomposition and the new
full-versus-routing-only comparison both identify this mechanism. This is a useful
form of resource allocation: no command is issued, and expensive unsuccessful work
is avoided. It is distinct from accelerating a task that the solver completes.

The distinction is quantitative here. Original jointly completed paths take longer
under \method, and the new jointly completed groups again pay additional wall time.
Panda joint-limit-return shows that successful-solve numerical savings do occur, but
the overall benefit is not uniform. Every-query candidate preparation and diagnostic
inference impose a fixed cost that a small reduction in FEV may not repay.
The correct comparison is the full returned-command pathway, with success and
failure handling reported alongside its cumulative cost.

\subsection{A verified current command need not preserve future recovery}

The verifier certifies the current pose, joint, and velocity conditions. It does not
certify that the resulting branch is favorable for later targets. The three lost
Panda reference paths make this distinction observable: different valid first
commands precede different later states, and the learned-entry paths eventually
exhaust the same robust stages that still succeed from hard's state. Disabling
rejection does not restore those completions.

Development-only temporal shortcuts likewise produced long runs of inexpensive
local solving but showed branch-dependent recovery and inconsistent cross-robot
behavior. These observations motivate examining the whole closed-loop command
sequence even for a policy that makes a fresh decision at each query. They are
mechanism observations, not an additional main method or a new temporal experiment.

\subsection{Practical position relative to an external solver}

The TRAC-IK comparison changes the deployment interpretation. Under the tested
exact-kinematics contract it generally returns feasible commands much faster than
the learned-proposal portfolio, and its new reference-path completion exceeds
full \method\ on both robots. \method\ retains a small UR5e point-success advantage
and completes two Panda paths missed by TRAC-IK, so the solution sets are not
identical. Those differences do not establish a general cost advantage.

This comparison includes implementation as well as numerical choices: native
parallel TRAC-IK and the Python/PyTorch portfolio have different execution costs.
For an application choosing between these measured systems, that cost is relevant.
For an algorithmic attribution, the six shared-solver controls are the cleaner
comparison. Taken together, they position \method\ as an interpretable study of
learned portfolio allocation and failure handling, not as a demonstrated replacement
for a mature general numerical IK solver.

\section{Limitations}

The command contract uses exact URDF forward kinematics and Jacobians. It excludes
collision, contact, dynamics, torque, sensing, tracking, acceleration, and jerk.
No physics simulator or physical robot is stepped. Zero accepted-contract violations
mean consistency with these explicit kinematic conditions, not universal safety.

A feasible reference path establishes existence from its own sequence of states.
Methods can leave that branch, so later frame inputs differ even under the same
target index. Only the point study holds complete inputs fixed throughout.
Outcome-conditioned trajectory groups describe observed cost and do not constitute
randomized causal subsets.

The study uses two robot-specific models and predetermined supplementary families,
not a prevalence-weighted deployment sample or cross-robot transfer test.
Forty trajectories per robot and ten per family provide limited precision when a
few paths dominate cumulative time. Paired bootstrap intervals preserve this
trajectory dependence but do not establish distribution-free success guarantees.
Five-repeat empirical P95 estimates are noisy; the independent-fold diagnostic
reduces selection bias without turning them into a true optimal-cost oracle.

Latency depends on the recorded hardware, synchronization, and implementations.
TRAC-IK uses Speed mode, its stated internal tolerance, and per-query limit updates;
a different configuration is a different comparison. Some nonnegative TRAC-IK
returns fail the common velocity check and remain failures: at the 5 ms point
budget there are 157 such Panda calls and three UR5e calls, plus one UR5e search
failure. The raw records retain those reasons. External FEV is not comparable.
No method's nominal command interval is treated as a measured real-time guarantee.

The frozen abstention rule also leaves many constructively inexecutable requests
on the conservative defer path, where they still incur numerical work. The
supplementary study does not retune that rule. Original OOD diagnostics and
operational criteria are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:originalboundaries};
no additional OOD experiment is introduced.

\section{Conclusions}

We studied whether learned query-conditioned costs can improve computation
allocation within a fixed IK portfolio. \method\ supplies an action-complete
learning formulation while preserving numerical command generation and
deterministic acceptance.

The evidence distinguishes useful failure handling from a general learned-entry
advantage. Original gains relative to fixed cascades remain intact, but their large
trajectory savings mostly occur on paths neither method completes. Supplementary
feasible-point controls do not outperform direct hard or a simple geometry rule;
P95 selection yields small FEV savings without a resolved tail advantage.
Continuously feasible paths retain a positive Panda joint-limit result, while
Panda near-singular completion losses and UR5e total-time overhead prevent a
cross-robot successful-path acceleration claim. TRAC-IK supplies a faster external
operating point with different completion coverage.

The hypothesis that robust coverage need not require uniform worst-case computation
therefore requires a more specific operational test than aggregate runtime alone.
In this portfolio, learned abstention avoids substantial unsuccessful work, whereas
learned entry selection does not establish the broader claimed benefit.
The architectural separation remains:
\emph{learning allocates solver effort per query; numerical geometry generates joint
commands; deterministic verification governs acceptance}.

\subsection{Original protocol boundaries}
\label{app:originalboundaries}

The original primary point system uses training seed \PointPrimaryTrainingSeed.

Prespecified point margins and a legacy trajectory-completion margin per robot
form one joint Holm family; all margin nulls were rejected.

in OOD-feasible false rejection. Panda fixed and \method\ both had zero such false

composite gate applied to the supplementary data.
